## [3] Sticking with a plan: the Google Maps rule
- **Figures.**
  - Fig 9 :41 "so keep every cut ranked and reversible".
  - Fig 8 :40 "Underivable-in-principle and unreachable-at-this-budget are different facts, and they issue different instructions."
  - Fig 12 :127 "the budget is what decides how much of the space is reached at all."
  - A plan that has stalled is unreachable at this budget, not refuted. So leaving it is reversible, and the deadline (the budget) sets how much delay is tolerable. Triangulated (Figs 9, 8, 12).
- **World.**
  - Cassey (Carwow, June 2026), on how Google Maps works: journey times come from speed limits, live speeds from other users, and historical patterns.
  - A diversion is offered only when the model predicts it gets you there sooner. That includes traffic that is clear now but usually heavy by the time you arrive.
  - Closures and accidents reroute the trip outright.
  - This corroborates Isaiah's version exactly: a projected finish compared with the alternative's projected finish, with a forecast of future delay, and a witness (a closed road) that switches at once.
- **Verdict (as ruled in r1306).** Compare the held plan's projected finish with the alternative's. The required saving shrinks as the deadline slack shrinks. Unknown routes carry an exploration cost.
- **Added by the world:** the forecast should use the agent's own history of how such lines usually go ("historical traffic"), not only the current rate.

## [1] Which guess first: the detective's board
- **Figures.**
  - Fig 1 :22 "Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average." So the readings (motive, opportunity, network, the cost of checking) are held apart.
  - Fig 9 :60 "Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict." The board orders the inquiry; only evidence convicts.
  - The formula's Direction note: "make the sought-for shape an ORDINARY TERM, write down what an answer would look like".
- **World.**
  - Dando & Ormerod (2017, *Human Factors*), on detectives' decision logs.
  - "experienced detectives display strategic decision-making to avoid confirmation and satisficing", and "Experienced detectives documented more hypotheses."
  - Less experienced detectives gathered as much evidence as fast as they could, which the authors tie to confirmation bias.
  - Writing the decision log down made an anomaly "more prominent" and changed one investigation.
- **Verdict.** Corroborated, with two additions from the world:
  - Keep several hypotheses alive. Do not fixate: fixation is Q3's failure in the detective's setting.
  - Writing the reasoning down (the narrator, the logged rows) is itself a reasoning aid, not just a record.
- This also supports Isaiah's 07:56 point that weight changes should be logged and shown.

## The searching budget: chess, and "just because it can doesn't mean it should"
- **Figures.**
  - Fig 9 :45 "Leaving, looking up and recognising are one step each, and cheap only in that order."
  - The formula, step 7: SEARCHED is "asked FIRST, cheapest, the others assume its answer".
  - Fig 4 :23: what crosses up is "a method that can be reapplied". An expert's chunks are carried methods.
  - So the agent recalls first and enumerates last. Triangulated (Fig 9, Fig 4, the formula).
- **World.**
  - Chase & Simon (1973, *Cognitive Psychology*, "Perception in Chess"): masters' advantage lies in "their ability to perceive structure in such positions and encode them in chunks".
  - De Groot found that masters examine about as many moves as weaker players, or fewer, but are very good at picking the right ones to consider.
  - Dando & Ormerod point the other way for hypotheses: generating many guards against fixation.
- **Verdict.** Both hold, at different levels:
  - Keep several **explanations** open, which is cheap because they are compared on readings.
  - Generate few **candidate actions**, led by recall (staged generation: recall, then kind composition, then enumeration as an escalation).
  - bp35's 1.7 million candidates are the level that should be small.

## [8] What to explore: the baby
- **Figures.**
  - The formula, step 2: TRANSFERRED means "nothing owed". The fully predictable is not worth a look.
  - Step 3's REACHABILITY gate: φ must lie in closure(Γ).
  - Fig 8 :40: unreachable at this budget is its own fact.
  - So the target is what is unexplained **and** reachable now, not the maximum of surprise. Triangulated (the formula, Fig 8).
- **World.**
  - Kidd, Piantadosi & Aslin (2012, *PLoS ONE*, "The Goldilocks Effect"): infants were most likely to look away from sequences whose complexity was "either very low or very high". They seek the middle.
  - Mather (2013, *Frontiers in Psychology*): novelty "can elicit activity and exploration" when the gap between what is known and what is found is large enough, best at a "moderate" gap.
  - Hunter & Ames' model, as Mather reports it: a familiarity preference comes first, and novelty is preferred once the familiar is encoded.
  - On needs overriding curiosity, Mather records the older drive theories (hunger, thirst) but says the mechanism is not yet understood. No source I read tests it in infants directly.
- **Verdict.** Isaiah's analogue is corroborated, and the world refines it:
  - Aim at moderate, learnable surprise, not the most surprising object. A board so complex the agent cannot model it is looked away from, then returned to.
  - Finish encoding the familiar before chasing the new.
  - "Needs come before curiosity" stands on the figures (stakes, A5). The world is silent on it here, not contrary.
- **This refines this morning's [8] ruling (r1306).** "What it understands least" becomes "the most unexplained among what is reachable now".

### [8] continued: needs before curiosity, as Maslow's hierarchy (Isaiah, 10:14: "survival > leisure")
- **Figures.**
  - Fig 10 :24 "the ground does not decay, the channel does".
  - The formula's closure note: the loop stops when R stops arriving, whether from "a perfect prediction or a closed channel".
  - An agent that loses the game (ruin) closes its channel for good, so every later lesson is lost with it. That is why survival comes before exploring, and why ruin is not traded one for one against an ordinary loss.
  - Triangulated (Fig 10, the formula's closure note).
- **World.**
  - Maslow (1943, *Psychological Review*): "these physiological needs are the most pre-potent of all needs". When a need is "fairly well satisfied, the next prepotent ('higher') need emerges".
  - But Maslow himself: "it is not nearly as rigid as we may have implied". People are "partially satisfied in all their basic needs and partially unsatisfied" at once.
  - Later tests did not find a strict ordering. Schimmack (2023), reviewing Wahba & Bridwell (1976) and later studies: "none of the ranking studies produced the predicted order". He calls the hierarchy "probabilistic and not deterministic".
  - Lima & Dill (1990, *Canadian Journal of Zoology*): animals trade predation risk against "the benefits to be gained from engaging in a given activity". The trade depends on state: "Hungry sticklebacks are apparently willing to accept the risk of feeding" on dense prey, while "partially satiated individuals are unwilling to take such a risk."
- **Verdict.**
  - Survival before leisure, as Isaiah says. It works as a **weighting by how pressing each need is (prepotency), not a fixed gate**. That matches both Maslow's own caveat and the audit's rule against fixed orders.
  - Curiosity (System 0) yields in proportion to how pressing a need is: the threat of ruin (lives running out), deadline pressure, a goal close to paying.
  - As the needs are fairly well met, exploring rises again.
  - Added from the animals: a desperate need also changes how much risk the agent accepts in serving it. A hungry agent near the deadline should take riskier lines toward the goal, and a comfortable one should not. That links to the deadline-slack factor in [3] and to the quit in Q18.
### [8] refined again: the dial (Isaiah, 10:19)
Isaiah: "probabilistic versus deterministic" is a dial problem. A starving person prioritises; someone recently fed, or able to eat readily, can forgo it (delayed gratification).
- **Figures.**
  - Symbols :24-25 "w is learnable from the outcomes of past weightings". The dial is a weight, set from the agent's own record, not a fixed order.
  - Fig 9 :41: the dial stays reversible as the state changes.
- **World.**
  - Skrynka & Vincent (2019, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*): "hunger causes large increases in delay discounting for food", "with an approximately 25% spillover effect to non-food commodities". So deprivation turns the dial toward now.
  - Kidd, Palmeri & Aslin (2013, *Cognition*, "Rational snacking"): children waited a mean of 12 min 2 s when the experimenter had been reliable, against 3 min 2 s when unreliable. The authors read the wait as reflecting "reasoned beliefs about whether waiting would ultimately pay off". So access, meaning trust that the need can be met later, turns the dial toward waiting.
  - Shah, Mullainathan & Shafir (2012; replicated 2019, *Journal of Economic Psychology*): scarcity "leads to attentional shifts and greater focus", but people "neglected predictable future demands" and "over-borrow". So a hard-turned dial has a cost: tunnel vision.
- **Verdict: Isaiah's dial, corroborated, with its two inputs named and one guard.**
  1. **Deprivation:** how far the agent is from meeting the need. Examples: lives left before ruin, deadline slack, and how far behind on progress it is.
  2. **Reliability:** how sure it is the need can be met later. This is the agent's own standing, its p, for the lines that serve that need. When reliability is high, it can afford to explore or wait (delayed gratification). When it is low, it acts on the need now.
  3. **The guard against tunnel vision:** even with the dial turned hard, future demands stay priced. Examples: actions the agent will need later, and lines it still owes. The leave and the alternative explanations stay open. Deprivation also spills over (the 25% effect), so a hungry dial should not reprice unrelated choices.
- **Sources:** [Skrynka & Vincent 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-019-01655-0) · [Kidd, Palmeri & Aslin 2013](https://www.kiddlab.com/_files/ugd/0975fd_d7e58cfd98604428945ba9afd6d41f64.pdf) · [Shah, Mullainathan & Shafir, replication 2019](https://shafir.scholar.princeton.edu/document/21)

- **Sources (Maslow section):** [Maslow 1943](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Maslow/motivation.htm) · [Schimmack 2023, on the empirical record](https://replicationindex.com/2023/12/18/maslows-hierarchy-of-needs-an-empirical-perspective/) · [Lima & Dill 1990](https://gambusia.zo.ncsu.edu/readings/Lima%20and%20Dill%201990.pdf)

## [2] and prep: the cook, the server
- **Figures.**
  - Fig 12 :126 "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."
  - Prep (priming) spends actions or time early to save them later, and is chosen when the clock affords it.
- **World.** Stevens (1993, *Applied Cognitive Psychology*), on skilled memory in waitresses:
  - they organised orders by "type of item, subclassified by location, such as hot or cold kitchen";
  - they used the world as a reminder ("empty sandwich plates may serve as reminders of desserts");
  - they "anticipated different use for the information at different points in their flow of activity", taking orders while already anticipating the payment.
- **Verdict.** Corroborated. Experts organise their working memory by where things are and what happens next. They let the board carry reminders, and they prepare ahead. Prep is an option chosen by the clock.
- **Added:** organise by location and next use, the way the ARC board's kinds and positions already key the agent's reads.

## [10] Pacing, tests, and gig work
- **Figures.** Fig 12 :125 "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add." Fig 12 :127 (the budget decides what is reached).
- **World.**
  - Davis (1991, Virginia Tech dissertation): telling students to skip hard items had "relatively little effect" overall. But high-ability students told **not** to skip scored lower. Weak corroboration: skipping helps those who can come back and use the time.
  - Gridwise (2025, driver data): experienced drivers judge each offer "on dollars per mile" against a floor, count "total time including the restaurant wait", and "Compare offers on pay per mile and pay per minute, not the headline dollar amount". Two currencies, judged as rates.
  - Charnov's marginal value theorem (already in the audit) generalises both: leave when the current rate falls below the habitat's average.
- **Verdict.** Pace by rates read from the agent's own record (bits per press, levels per second), held apart, never summed. Skip and return when the order allows it (non-sequential). In sequential games, the move-on decision is the leave rule.
