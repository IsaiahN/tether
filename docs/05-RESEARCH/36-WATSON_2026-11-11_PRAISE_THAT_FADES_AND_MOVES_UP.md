My 06:50 line is WITHDRAWN. The figures and the world both side with Isaiah: second nature does not form from unrewarded repetition. It forms in three stages:
1. Pay is large while the act is new for this self.
2. The pay shrinks as the act becomes expected.
3. The act becomes a part that bigger goals are built on, and the pay moves up to those bigger goals.

QUESTION: How does an act move from praised to expected (second nature), and what does the agent need for the same path?

WORLD (secondary, each source read):
- Neuroscience (signal mellowing with experience). Schultz, Dayan & Montague, Science 275:1593, 1997, Fig. 1 caption:
  - an unpredicted reward: "The dopamine neuron is activated by this unpredicted occurrence of juice";
  - after learning: "the primary reward no longer elicits a phasic response";
  - instead, "the onset of the (predictive) light now causes a phasic activation";
  - on omission: "The activity of the dopamine neuron is depressed exactly at the time when the reward would have occurred."
  - Predicts three things:
    - (a) the delight fades as the act becomes expected;
    - (b) the signal moves earlier and upward, to what predicts the payoff, so the mastered act becomes a cue for the bigger goal;
    - (c) once expected, success is silent and failure stands out (an adult with untied shoes gets noticed).
  - Corroborates Fig 5 :11, Fig 4 :24 and Fig 11 :49.
- Child development (scaffolding). Wood, Bruner & Ross, J. Child Psychol. Psychiatry 17(2):89-100, 1976, via the Structural Learning briefing "Support that fades":
  - the tutor had "no fixed script; each child's own success or failure decided her next move";
  - "Her help fell away as each child's own skill grew".
  - The briefing notes the study describes this pattern without a control group.
  - Predicts: the support is contingent and withdrawn with competence. Corroborates Fig 11 :70 / :73. The source does not mention praise itself.
- Child development (expectations by age). Havighurst, Developmental Tasks and Education (1948), via Wrosch, "Developmental Tasks", Encyclopedia of Aging (Gale):
  - "A developmental task is one that arises predictably and consistently at or about a certain period in the life of the individual";
  - early tasks: "learning to walk, to take solid food, and to control the elimination of body wastes";
  - adolescence: "preparing for an occupation".
  - Success supports later tasks; failure brings "social disapproval".
  - Predicts: each age has its own expected tasks, and a mastered task is no longer praised; failing at it draws disapproval. Corroborates Isaiah's "replaced with larger and more abstract expectations" and Schultz's (c).
- Child motivation (why praise must stop). Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, J. Pers. Soc. Psychol., 1973: "subjects in the expected-award condition would show less subsequent intrinsic interest in the target activity."
  - Predicts: a reward that keeps coming once expected has a cost; it crowds out the drive. Corroborates the withdrawal, and warns against paying the agent forever for what it has mastered.
- Animals (training). Patterson, AKC, "How to Successfully Fade the Lure", 2025:
  - early: "gets that food reward each time they perform the new behavior";
  - then "Once your dog is consistently performing the behavior, you can then begin to vary your rate of reinforcement";
  - and finally "your dog should still get praise—just maybe not with a treat each time."
  - Predicts the same path as the toddler: reward every step, then fade, then occasional praise. Corroborates.
- Animals (what a habit is). de Wit et al., J. Exp. Psychol. Gen. 147(7), 2018, citing Adams & Dickinson 1981 and Adams 1982:
  - "following moderate training, rats are capable of goal-directed, purposeful, control over action";
  - "following extensive training, rats will persist in lever pressing for the devalued food reward", so the behaviour "is no longer under goal-directed control".
  - Predicts: second nature runs without consulting the reward. It is cheap, but it persists after the payoff is gone. Corroborates Isaiah's 03:03 "second nature is only as good as it is useful": a habit needs a way to be re-judged. The agent has one in the store clock's reassessment.
- Machine learning (curiosity). Schmidhuber, "Driven by Compression Progress", arXiv:0812.4360, 2008:
  - "This drive maximizes interestingness, the first derivative of subjective beauty or compressibility";
  - "It will get bored by things that already are subjectively compressible".
  - Predicts: the drive leaves the mastered and goes to what can still be learned. Corroborates Fig 5 :15-18 on AIM.
  - Partial conflict, resolved by domain: Schmidhuber counts compression progress as reward. The Tether counts "compression achieved" as frame-internal, never the metric (Fig 1 :25-27). So in the agent, learning progress may aim the search (curiosity) but never score it (the ground). Re-censused: the conflict is the aim domain against the ground domain, not a contradiction.

HOW ANIMALS DIFFER FROM PEOPLE (from the sources read):
- Who pays early:
  - a child is paid by people (praise, a social currency), and later by society's expectations for each age (Havighurst);
  - a dog is paid by a trainer in food, faded to occasional praise;
  - a rat in the lab is paid by the experimenter.
  - In each case a substituted habitat pays first (Fig 11 :70), and the real consequence takes over later.
- What replaces the praise:
  - for people, larger and more abstract expectations (school, work, roles), which are socially set;
  - for the animals in these sources, nothing larger: the act is kept up by occasional reinforcement or by habit.
  - The abstract ladder is the human difference. In the Tether that ladder is composition: the mastered act becomes a part of a larger term (Fig 4, Fig 5 :70).
- What failure costs once the act is expected: a dip in all three (Schultz's omission response; Havighurst's disapproval; a dog's missed treat).

WHAT THE AGENT ALREADY HAS (gamma.py, read now):
- Pay shrinks as the act becomes expected: confidence = (confirmations + 1) / (confirmations + refusals + 2) (gamma.py:1722, :1751). One more success raises it by exactly (1 − confidence) / (n + 3), where n is the trials so far. So the bump is the surprise left (1 − confidence) times a learning rate that shrinks. This is the prediction-error shape Schultz measured: big when new, near zero when expected.
- Success on an expected act owes nothing (Fig 5 :11): a held prediction is not a confirmation.
- Failure of an expected act is news: a refusal of a high-confidence term drops it by more, and the store clock re-judges it (reassessment, r1713).
- The curiosity drive aims at large, compressible gaps.

WHAT MAY BE MISSING (to measure, not yet claimed):
1. **Does the pay move up?** Once a term is settled, does it become a part that larger terms compose over? And does the goal proposer stop aiming at it as a goal and start using it as a step? A child praised forever for tying shoes would be the failure: the agent re-proposing a mastered act as its goal instead of building on it. (Fig 5 :70: settled is not usable.)
2. **Early encouragement for partial steps (shaping).** Under Fig 1 :23-27 the agent may not praise itself. Step-by-step encouragement has to come from outside: the ground, or a maintainer's curriculum (a substituted habitat), withdrawn as competence grows. In the build, that is the curriculum's job (Phase 2 walkthroughs, the harness), not a self-reward inside the agent.
